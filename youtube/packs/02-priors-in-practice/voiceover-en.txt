You downloaded reinvent_pubchem.prior. Congrats — that is not the same as choosing a prior for your chemotype.

Tutorial 01 got you a working environment and a real sampled.csv. That prior was trained on a broad chemical distribution — think PubChem-scale grammar. Your project is not PubChem. Kinase hinges, macrocycles, covalent warheads, natural-product-like scaffolds — if your chemotype is rare in the pretraining soup, the model will still smile and emit SMILES. Validity is not relevance.

Three words every README skips. The prior is the frozen pretrained brain — chemical grammar. The agent starts as a copy you will later train with rewards. The vocabulary is the alphabet inside the prior file. In this chapter we are not training yet. We are asking: which frozen brain should we even start from?

Rule one: same protocol, different brains. Fix the seed, the number of samples n, and the device. Sample from at least two priors — or one public prior versus a transfer-learning checkpoint. If you change seed and model at once, you learned nothing. Reuse the Tutorial 01 sampling config shape. Only swap model_file.

Then measure like an experimentalist. Validity percent. Unique Bemis-Murcko scaffolds — not just unique SMILES. Simple property histograms: molecular weight, logP, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, maybe QED. You want coverage of your region without collapsing into one scaffold family. Pretty molecules from the wrong neighborhood lose later — in scoring and in reinforcement learning.

Write this down: when should you not start from a PubChem-style prior? Domain shift. Rare chemotypes. Project libraries that live far from drug-like averages. In those cases, a smaller in-domain prior — or a transfer-learning warm start — can beat a famous public file. Popularity is not a control experiment.

So the research practice is simple. Compare. Measure. Decide with evidence. Then lock the prior before you burn GPU on reinforcement learning.
